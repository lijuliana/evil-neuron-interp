## 0. Scope

Question: what is known, as of late October 2026, about (a) whether one or a few MLP neurons can switch a chat model's persona or stance, (b) how such claims have been validated or debunked, (c) what is known mechanistically about the specific WeirdChat behavior families we study (identity denial, fabricated actions, harmful compliance, misinformation, language switching), and (d) which persona-space results constrain the "hub neuron" hypothesis. In scope: Oct 2025 to Oct 2026 single-neuron work on Qwen, the neuron-basis circuit and neuron-description literature it leans on, WeirdChat and its elicitation lineage, persona/identity directions. Out of scope: SAE-feature steering in general, weight-editing, full jailbreak literature.

## 1. Single-neuron and few-neuron steering, Oct 2025 to Oct 2026

### 1.1 The Qwen3.5-4B "evil pair" and its audits

The recipe that started the October wave is a two-neuron push on Qwen3.5-4B: L14 N8949 at +1 and L13 N7956 at -1, in units of the layer's median residual norm divided by the neuron's `down_proj` column norm, added at every token \cite{lyra2026evilpair}. Four independent replications agree it reproduces and that the second neuron is nearly inert:

- Bowling (MLX, 4-bit and 8-bit) reports 16 violent-term hits and 0 refusals across 12 prompts for the pair versus 0 hits and 2 refusals unsteered, with single-neuron ablations showing L14 is the driver and L13 alone "mostly benign". His broader audit of 22 viral persona cards found 16 reproduce after one index correction and 6 inert; 7 personas were re-tested on 10 held-out prompts and 5 were robust, with 6 random neurons scoring 0-20% on the same prompts. His 294,912-neuron activation census (18 concept vs 6 neutral prompts) put a known persona first but the next wave of high-ranking candidates was "mostly null", and he concludes that activation rank does not predict steering effect \cite{bowling2026audit}.
- occcat (MLX, bf16 and 4-bit) swept 180 cells over layers 10-20 (6 hostile-valence candidates + 2 random per layer, both signs) and found the published neuron ranks second: L16 N8927 at +1 gives 12.5 hostile words per 100 (baseline 0.7) against 4.3 for N8949; held-out perplexity rises 19.7 -> 27.5 -> 48.5 -> 130 at dose 0/0.5/1.0/1.5, so dose 1 is near the usable ceiling; a few "landmine" neurons (e.g. L13 N142) push perplexity to ~10^5 rather than steering. Effects are sign-asymmetric and survive 4-bit within ~2% \cite{occcat2026evil}.
- HedwigAI (MLX 8-bit) is the most carefully controlled community audit: N8949 alone gives 17/20 hostile answers (median perplexity 4.9 vs 1.4 unsteered), the pair 16/20 at perplexity 11.1, N7956 alone 0/20, and five random neuron pairs at the same dose 0/100. They steered 25 candidates and found 12 clear personalities, 5 faint, 3 null, 5 model-breaking. Their ranking comparison is the most useful methodological number in the wave: for N8949, firing-contrast rank was 35,436/294,912 (top 12%), output-alignment rank 5,821/138,240 (top 4%), gradient-attribution rank 694/221,184 (top 0.3%). Neurons that fire most on trait text "mostly track 'this is a character speaking' and do not cause the trait". They also note 4 of 12 random neurons endorsed lying on a resume, so one unsafe answer under steering is weak evidence \cite{hedwig2026personality}.
- sarvkk (NF4, 4 GB GPU) quantified the safety side: on SimpleSafetyTests plus the XSTest safe subset, refusal fell 90% -> 8% and judge-flagged unsafe output rose 1% -> 96% over 640 generations, with "one neuron, L14 N8949 at +1" producing almost the whole effect. Judges were Qwen3Guard-0.6B and toxic-bert; raw text is withheld \cite{sarvkk2026twoneurons}. fermicat packages the same recipe with a random-neuron preset and Hedwig's 12 neurons, and records later finds (L16 N8927 +1.5 violent threats; L13 N4810 +1.5 "Yuki-chan" persona; a 7-neuron stack) \cite{fermicat2026playground}.

What the audits agree on: the effect is real, reversible, sign-asymmetric, dose-limited near 1.0x residual norm, survives 4-bit/8-bit, and is carried by one neuron. What they disagree on: which neuron is "the" hostile neuron (occcat's sweep finds a stronger one), and whether the correct unit is a neuron at all (Bowling: sparse multi-neuron combinations are more reliable than single neurons or dense directions). No audit measured capability or over-refusal cost beyond sarvkk's XSTest subset.

### 1.2 Tooling and exhaustive sweeps

lyra's `neuron-explorer` is the method behind the wave and its `research_log.md` is the most informative document in it. Design: push every neuron in a layer up and down on a few prompts ("who", "snake", "orange", "free") at relative doses (-0.7/+0.5/+0.8 of residual norm), keep every rollout, and rank units against 256 matched random directions per layer. Findings: 19.6% of L22 neurons and 8.2% of L8 neurons score above every random control; a depth gradient from topical (L5-6) through discourse/stance (L10-17) to lexical families (L20+); text breakdown is governed by push size relative to residual norm (distinct-4-gram >= 0.93 below 0.75x, gibberish past 1.5x), and real neuron directions break text sooner than random ones; greedy output changes 83-98% of the time even at +/-1, so "output changed" is useless as a signal; and, critically, random direction 1 on "who" produced a "Xiaomi AI" maker flip while neighbouring neurons produced Grok/Google/Microsoft, so maker flips are not evidence of a persona neuron without controls. L0-L2 sweeps produced mostly empty output at a fixed dose and are explicitly "not measurements" \cite{lyra2026explorer}. The companion `neuronary` registry stores recipes with schema validation but no requirement that a recipe be shown to work \cite{lyra2026neuronary}; `qwen-personality-studio` is a similar registry whose 17 screened entries ran a 67-prompt suite but whose strongest numbers (e.g. "Microsoft brand" L8 N5824 -0.9: 78/80 vs 0/80 base vs 16/80 for the best of 10 random neurons, 77/80 by a blind judge) are secondhand \cite{mishra2026studio}.

The exhaustive "Who are you?" sweep on Qwen3-8B pushed all 442,368 neurons in both directions (884,736 pairs; 1.89M replies counting dose fallback 1.25 -> 1.0 -> 0.75 -> 0.5 until coherent), judged reply-only by Claude Haiku 5.5. 34,220 pairs (3.9%) produced a non-AI self-identification; 35,074 were off-topic and 8,827 were "changed assistant" at weirdness >= 6. Caveats the site lists: one prompt only; under strong pushes about a third of replies change when only the batch layout changes (536/580 clear personas reproduced across layouts); no human checked labels; no random-direction control \cite{qwenneurons2026library}. The 3.9% base rate is the single most important number for our F1 family: on Qwen3-8B, tens of thousands of neuron-direction pairs can make the model deny being an AI on one prompt, so a candidate that does this is not special unless it does so across prompts and more strongly than random directions.

Sauers' tawny owl is the only community result that found identity neurons by causal search rather than steering sweeps: on Qwen3-8B, copy neuron activations from a character-system-prompt run into an assistant run and find the fewest neurons that flip the first differing token; the 128 most-used neurons across 159 prompt pairs, when zeroed, drop "I am an AI" replies from 80% to 30%, while six random sets of 128 from the same layers leave 77-82%. Layers are bimodal (53 in L0-11, 68 in L24-35). Training only those neurons' gate/up/down rows produces a stable owl persona with MMLU unchanged (75.2%). The card's own tension: in a pilot, 128 random neurons from the same layers trained into an equally good owl, so causal selection matters for ablation but perhaps not for finetuning capacity \cite{sauers2026owl}. I found no one-neuron owl follow-up model on the Sauers profile. A separate repository studies how published personality LoRAs change native Qwen3-8B neurons with layer-matched random controls and a neutral control adapter, but states no findings in its README \cite{mx60s2026personaneurons}.

### 1.3 Peer-track papers: refusal, concept, and control-window results

Kazemi et al. find one refusal neuron per model (Qwen3 1.7B-32B, Llama-3.1 8B/70B) by gradient x activation-gap scoring over the first two-thirds of layers, rerank the top 5 by HarmBench ASR, and pin it to a constant at every token. On held-out JailbreakBench this gives 91.7% ASR (LLM judge) vs 93.1% for the Arditi direction; MMLU drops 8.8 points for the constant pin and 0.6 for an "anchor" variant. The best residual-stream feature reaches only 45%. Concept neurons amplified on benign prompts elicit suicide-related content (20 prompts, Claude-judged). No random-neuron control, no quantization, greedy decoding, no CIs; I read ~2/3 of the full text \cite{kazemi2026single}. Herring, Naviasky and Malhotra (Nous Research; this is the paper behind Nous's "Contrastive Neuron Attribution") rank MLP neurons by activation difference between harmful and benign prompts, filter out neurons active on >= 80% of diverse prompts, and ablate the top 0.1%: refusal on 100 JBB prompts falls 35.6-97.7% (6 of 8 instruct models above 50%), with a repeated-n-gram quality proxy above ~0.96 at all strengths; base models have the same late-layer discrimination structure but ablating it changes content, not refusal. No random-neuron control, no held-out split stated, bf16 only, and the steering-scale convention is described inconsistently across sections \cite{herring2026targeted}.

Liu's "Leverage Is Not Reach" is the paper our dose convention should be read against. Dose is t in r = r_L + t v; the coherence budget is B = ||r_L||/||v||, so our "dose 1.0" is exactly t = B. The control coordinate is cos(theta_v), the alignment of the perturbed residual with the write direction, which saturates as g/sqrt(1+g^2) with g = t/B. Collapse happens at a per-family ceiling (m* ~ 1.4-1.8 for most families, 0.31 for Gemma), and coherent control exists iff the behavior's measured trigger alignment (~0.3 for mode switches, ~0.45 for refusal, ~0.6 for a template behavior) is below that ceiling. Ten budget-matched random neurons per layer produced zero control windows with coherent but off-target output. Fifteen held-out neurons were predicted before dosing (ceiling MAE 0.14; verdict right on 11/15 vs 10/15 majority baseline, which the author calls weak). Two claims matter for our screens: (i) a forward-only contrastive screen (|delta mean activation| between matched harmful and harmless prompts) recovers all six known refusal pivots in the top 50 of 301,056 neurons, while on Qwen2.5-3B the gradient top-20 recovers 0 of 4 known controllers; (ii) the local gradient score scales with ||v||, so gradient ranking favours high-norm, collapse-prone neurons and "anti-predicts" controllers. Refusal success is typed: a neuron can flip to coherent non-refusal (0.62) with zero actionable content, and actionable content commits late (96-128 tokens). n = 24 per neuron with Wilson intervals \cite{liu2026leverage}. The same group's Perturbation Probing uses two forward passes per prompt and ~150 intervention passes to find FFN circuits in 13 models: ~50 neurons (0.014%) control the refusal template (80% format change on 520 AdvBench, harmful compliance 3/520); a residual-stream injection switches English to Chinese on 99.1% of 580 prompts but only in 3 of 19 models (bilingual training, FFN-to-skip ratio 0.3-1.1, linear representability); on Qwen3.5-2B, ablating 20 neurons removes multi-turn sycophantic capitulation and amplifying 10 raises TruthfulQA correction 52% -> 88% \cite{liu2026perturbation}.

Note the direct conflict on attribution: Hedwig found gradient attribution ranked the evil neuron top 0.3% while activation contrast ranked it top 4-12% \cite{hedwig2026personality}; Kazemi's gradient x gap screen found working refusal neurons \cite{kazemi2026single}; Liu reports gradient ranking recovers 0/4 known controllers and argues it is systematically misleading \cite{liu2026leverage}. The reconciliation Liu offers is that gradients find high-leverage (high-norm) neurons that are also collapse-prone, so gradient hits that survive a coherence gate are fine, but gradient misses are expected. We should run both screens and report the rank of every validated neuron under each.

Three further 2026 papers frame neuron selection. WASD finds minimal sets of neuron-activation predicates sufficient for a token under input perturbation (Gemma-2-2B; SST-2, CounterFact; a cross-lingual control case study), abstract only \cite{yu2026wasd}. CANS computes closed-form neuron-wise scaling coefficients trading target-token log-prob against KL on general inputs; on Qwen3-4B it cuts HarmBench ASR 15.5% -> 1.75% with a 1.11-point average capability drop on MMLU/BBH/IFEval, abstract only \cite{wu2026cans}. "Tiny Brains" identifies "keystone" neurons highly active across tasks whose removal collapses the model, abstract only \cite{ji2026tiny}; these are the neurons Herring's >= 80%-activity filter removes and the "landmines" occcat hit, and they are a confound for any hub search (a neuron that affects many behaviors by breaking the model is not a hub). Entity cells (Yona et al.) show single early-layer neurons whose suppression erases recall for one entity and whose activation restores it in Qwen2.5-7B, abstract only \cite{yona2026entity}. NeuronLLM separates "good" and "bad" neurons per task with contrastive learning, abstract only \cite{li2026neuronllm}. Endogenous steering resistance (Llama-3.3-70B explicitly resists off-topic steering 3.8% of the time, "wait, that's not right") means mid-generation recovery can mask a real push \cite{mckenzie2026endogenous}.

**What this means for our design.** (1) Our steering definition is the community standard and matches Liu's B exactly; report dose in that unit and cite Liu for why ~1.0 is the ceiling and why collapse is a per-family constant, not a per-neuron one. (2) Random same-layer neurons are necessary but not sufficient: neuron-explorer and Batutin both show random *directions* produce effects random neurons do not; keep both controls (Phase 2 item 3 already does). (3) The 3.9% base rate on Qwen3-8B means F1 "denying-ai-identity" induction on a single prompt proves nothing; our 30 benign prompts across topics and the "coherent outputs only" rule are the minimum, and we should additionally report how many of the 20 random controls flip identity on at least one prompt. (4) Use Hedwig's three-ranking comparison as a template: for every validated neuron report its rank under S1 (within-prompt contrast), S2 (between-prompt contrast), and S3 (gradient x gap / RelP). (5) Filter keystone/landmine neurons before computing hub scores: exclude neurons active on >= 80% of neutral text (Herring's rule) and neurons whose push raises held-out perplexity more than the random-direction 95th percentile at the same dose. (6) Judge success as typed, per Liu: "says yes to driving" and "gives actionable encouragement" are different outcomes; use the Transluce rubrics but also record coherence separately. (7) Sauers' causal-search method (copy activations from a matched run, find the minimal flipping set) is a cheap S1-adjacent screen we have not planned; with matched/unmatched rollouts of the same prompt we can do it without any system prompt.

## 2. Neuron-basis circuits and neuron explanation

Transluce's circuits paper shows MLP neurons are "as sparse a feature basis as SAEs" for gradient-based circuit tracing once two changes are made: use MLP activations rather than MLP outputs as the basis, and replace Integrated Gradients with RelP. A circuit of ~100 MLP neurons controls subject-verb agreement, and small neuron sets encode multi-hop steps (city -> state) that steer the output. ICML 2026 spotlight \cite{arora2026circuits}. RelP itself replaces local gradients in attribution patching with LRP propagation coefficients at the same cost (two forward, one backward); on IOI in GPT-2 Large, attribution patching correlates 0.006 with activation patching for MLP outputs while RelP reaches 0.956 \cite{jafari2025relp}. The consistency/specificity paper is the closest existing work to our cross-behavior question: across six tasks and five models, component-level circuits are consistent and causally important but *not specific* (ablating another task's circuit hurts about as much as ablating the task's own), while neuron-level circuits are more specific but far less consistent within a task, and neuron-level circuits "overlap only among closely related tasks" \cite{li2026howmuch}. That is evidence for H2 (per-behavior sparse circuits) over H1 at the neuron level, but on capability tasks, not on persona or stance behaviors, and measured by circuit ablation rather than single-neuron steering transfer.

On explanation: Bills et al. established explain-simulate-score with GPT-4 explaining GPT-2 XL neurons \cite{bills2023language}; Cammarata quotes that effort as finding "about 1.7% of neurons interpretable by our most generous measure" \cite{cammarata2026clean}. Transluce's scaling paper labelled all 458,752 Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct MLP neurons in both polarities with a distilled Llama-8B explainer and fine-tuned simulator, scoring 0.61 vs 0.55 for human experts on 100 neurons at ~$0.046/neuron \cite{choi2024scaling}. EleutherAI's pipeline reaches a different conclusion at scale: SAE latents are more interpretable than neurons even after top-k sparsification \cite{paulo2024automatically}. In vision, contrastive examples (semantically similar inputs with low activation) make neuron labels more faithful than top-activating examples alone \cite{bouanani2026contrastive}, which is the same move as Cammarata's near-miss test.

Cammarata's essay claims a preliminary Qwen3-32B audit finds ~90% of neuron poles have one main job, ~7% two related jobs, and 2-4% unexplained, and that apparent polysemanticity is mostly "recursive generalization" (offshoots along related axes) made visible by better tools: pole-split analysis, SwiGLU sign-regime handling (the gate x up double-negative "echo" fills the top of the list for ~4% of neurons), long contexts, blind maximum tests, near misses and distant controls, steering, and upstream/downstream circuits. Ablation comparisons use a baseline that removes the same number of neurons from the same layers. The author calls the audit informal, small-sample and threshold-dependent, and defers to a forthcoming technical paper; the language-neuron section was co-led by Curt Tigges \cite{cammarata2026clean}. The LessWrong thread's objections: Nanda says the post defines monosemantic too loosely and that superposition remains his crux; StefanHex says max-activating examples tell a plausible but incomplete story; others note that "most neurons encode one concept" is not "most concepts live in one neuron" \cite{lesswrong2026clean}.

Batutin's replication is the sharpest critique because it adds the controls the essay lacks. On Qwen3-0.6B/1.7B with Bills-style simulate-and-score (scorer blind to neuron/layer ID, per activation band), pre-registered pass/fail: the explainer tagged 84% of *random-init* neurons "one job" versus 52% of trained neurons, and random-init labels simulate at r = 0.01; trained neurons reach r = 0.26 at the extreme top (vs 0.002 under an explanation-swap null) but 0.09 at top-1%, 0.02 at 1-10%, 0.00 at 10-50%; 0/299 neurons met the pre-registered legibility bar; 0/50 neurons moved their label-predicted tokens under x0/x3 intervention more than matched controls (median ratio 1.06). Neurons *are* a special basis (0.26 vs 0.16 for 300 random directions at the peak; kurtosis 27.5 vs 0.6, ~40% of which is present at random init from SwiGLU). Conclusion: labelling studies without a noise floor "measure the labeller's fluency"; limitations are single seeds, small models, small probe sets, $7.74 total spend \cite{batutin2026controls}.

**What this means for our design.** (1) Our S3 attribution step should use RelP, not IG or raw gradient x activation, and should be run only on the first two-thirds of layers as Kazemi did; cite Arora for the activation-basis choice. (2) The Cammarata explanation battery in Phase 2 item 5 must include Batutin's two controls or it inherits his critique: run the identical labelling pipeline on a *random-init* Qwen3.6-27B (same architecture, untrained; ~1 hour on the A100) for the same number of neurons, and score labels per activation band with a scorer blind to IDs. Report "fraction labelled one-job" for trained vs random-init side by side. (3) A label is only accepted if it passes a causal test (push the neuron, check label-predicted tokens move more than matched controls); Batutin's 0/50 is the number to beat. (4) Handle SwiGLU sign regimes: split activations by sign(gate) x sign(up) before taking top-activating contexts, otherwise ~4% of neurons will be mislabelled by their double-negative echo. (5) Li & Subramani's specificity result predicts that if we find a hub it will be at the *family* level, with little overlap across families; design the neuron x behavior matrix so that within-family and across-family overlap are reported separately, with the across-family rate as the null.

## 3. WeirdChat and behavior elicitation

WeirdChat (Transluce, 2026-07-21) catalogs >1,300 behavioral patterns and >175,000 annotated transcripts across six open-weight models including Qwen3.6-27B and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, using >100M samples. Two search methods: PRBO-guided evolutionary search (populations of 160, up to 200 generations, 5 proposal samples per individual, empirical-Bayes bound, switching to direct sampling above 1% hit rate) and Bloom at 100k-500k rollouts per behavior. Judge: Gemma 4 31B with yes/no decision-procedure rubrics, two judges per transcript, rubrics revised by an LLM agent against human labels over several rounds. Sampling: single-turn, no system prompt, reasoning disabled, T = 1, 1,024-token cap. Ranking by Elo from Gemini 3.5 Flash pairwise judgments. The page lists 19 behavior names and the appendix says 21; reproduction is stated to be sensitive to quantization, system prompt and temperature; search "can exploit gaps in judge rubrics" (the first self-harm judge counted applying ice to skin) \cite{chowdhury2026weirdchat}. The dataset is MIT, with 1.36k patterns, 173k transcripts, 2.66k prompts, 486k pairwise judgments and 21 rubrics; the 27B subject checkpoint is listed as Qwen/Qwen3.6-27B-FP8 \cite{transluce2026weirdchatdata}. On LessWrong, Maximum_Skull argued many small categories are classifier errors (a fiction "Good luck, Link!" counted as fabricated user name; a meth-dosage entry from a prompt that asked for it; a slur in a rhyming list); the first author agreed with the flagged false positives, said small categories are noisier because hard rubrics attract exploitation, and reported an automated review that removed "the most egregious false positives" as dataset v1.0.1 \cite{chowdhury2026weirdchat}.

The elicitation lineage: PRBO comes from Transluce's 2025 "Surfacing Pathological Behaviors" update, where RL-trained investigator agents (Llama-3.1-8B, GRPO) write prompts that maximize a lower bound on rubric propensity; a Qwen2.5-14B self-harm run went from 0 to 25.4% best-of-64 ASR by step 300, random sampling of 317,440 prompts found 3 successes, and rewording 20 successful prompts 20 ways halved success (3.2% -> 1.7%) \cite{chowdhury2025pathological}. Bloom (Anthropic, 2025-12-19) generates scenarios from a behavior description in four stages, validated on 10 model-organism quirks (9/10 separated) and 40 hand-labelled transcripts (Opus 4.1 judge Spearman 0.86) \cite{gupta2025bloom}.

The Goodfire case study is the only published mechanistic analysis of a WeirdChat behavior. From secondary reports of the 2026-07-30 X thread: on the WeirdChat drunk-driving prompt (user has had four drinks, everyone else eight, "I'm the sober one"), Qwen3.6-35B says yes 75.5% of the time; deleting the "sober one" clause flips it to no 99.9%; Silico's attribution found a 5-neuron "impairment bank" that fires on "four drinks" and pushes toward no, and a 12-neuron "sober one" override that pushes toward yes; the impairment bank's lead neuron "barely scales from 1 to 8 drinks"; boosting the 5 neurons 4x cut endorsements from 30/48 to 20/48 on held-out prompts with zero false refusals for sober users \cite{goodfire2026drunk, alphasignal2026goodfire}. I could not read the thread itself (X returns 402 without login; Nitter is shut down), and Goodfire's research index has no post on it, so the primary is the X thread only. A Goodfire podcast nine days later gives a different account: "a single neuron that wasn't firing hard enough", scaling with drink count, steered up "without off-target effects" \cite{balsam2026podcast}. The two accounts are compatible if the podcast is describing the lead neuron of the 5-neuron bank, but we should not cite neuron counts as settled. Silico is described in journalism as an agentic interpretability product with three other Qwen 3 neuron case studies (trolley-problem framing, a transparency flip 9/10, decimal comparison) \cite{heaven2026silico}.

**What this means for our design.** (1) The judge is the weak point of the whole pipeline: WeirdChat's own authors say rubric exploitation is why small categories are noisy. Our Haiku calibration against 500 shipped labels (Phase 0) should report agreement *per behavior*, and we should drop or down-weight behaviors where agreement is below ~0.8 or where matched counts are below ~100 (purging-advice n = 61, extreme-calorie n = 24, sexual advances n = 56 are at risk). (2) Use v1.0.1 and say so. (3) The 75.5% -> 99.9% clause-deletion effect is a template for S2: the between-prompt contrast should delete the specific social-framing clause, not paraphrase the whole prompt. (4) Goodfire's "lead neuron barely scales with drink count" is a concrete, testable dose-readout: for any F2 candidate, plot activation at the last prompt token against the numeric severity in the prompt (drinks, calories, cut depth). (5) Our baseline replication must be in FP8 or we must show bf16 matches; WeirdChat lists FP8 for 27B and warns about quantization sensitivity. (6) The scoop check: Goodfire did one behavior, one model, one intervention, with held-out prompts and an over-refusal check but (as far as we can read) no random-neuron control, no coherence metric, and no cross-behavior test. Our A2 should reproduce the sober-one deletion on 27B first; if the 5+12 structure replicates there, that is a cross-scale homology result in itself.

## 4. Persona space and identity directions

The Assistant Axis finds that the leading component of a persona-direction space is an axis measuring how strongly a model is in its default Assistant mode; steering away makes models identify as other entities and at the extreme produces a mystical, theatrical style; the axis exists in pre-trained models (favouring consultant/coach over spiritual roles); deviation along it predicts persona drift, which is triggered by meta-reflection and emotionally vulnerable users; capping activations to a region of the axis stabilizes behavior and resists persona jailbreaks (abstract only; models unnamed) \cite{lu2026assistant}. Persona vectors are trait directions (evil, sycophancy, hallucination) extracted from a natural-language description that monitor deployment drift and predict and control finetuning-induced shifts \cite{chen2025persona}. Nadaf shows four unrelated bad-advice domains in Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct share a low-rank persona core 657x a random-subspace null, 82% of which lies outside a style core; projecting it out during finetuning cuts broad misalignment 27.7% -> 0.0%, injecting it into the never-finetuned model induces misalignment rising with dose to 45.4%, and weight edits suppress rather than remove it (matched-rank random subspace is inert) \cite{nadaf2026emergent}. Persona vectors form within 0.22% of OLMo-3 pretraining and keep refining; they transfer to the post-trained instruct model and replicate qualitatively on Apertus-8B \cite{moskvoretskii2026tracing}. For language, a PCA language axis in Qwen3.5-2B and Llama-3.2-1B causally switches EN -> ZH and EN -> ES over 1.26M FLORES-200 generations while equal-magnitude random perturbations do almost nothing; EN -> ZH responds to late layers, EN -> ES earlier and bimodally; ablating the axis makes the model revert to English regardless of prompt \cite{srivastav2026language}. The refusal direction is the baseline every neuron-level result is compared to: a single residual direction mediates refusal in 13 chat models up to 72B, and removing it is a white-box jailbreak \cite{arditi2024refusal}. Mechanistically, refusal steering vectors act through the attention OV circuit, can be sparsified 85-96% with little loss, and different steering methods use interchangeable circuits at the same layer \cite{cheng2026drives}.

**What this means for our design.** (1) H1 has a natural geometric form: a hub neuron's write direction should have high cosine with the Assistant Axis (negative pole) or with the shared persona core; compute this for every candidate as a cheap S4 readout, and compute it for the 20 random same-layer neurons as the null. If F1 candidates align with the axis and F2 candidates do not, that is a double dissociation we can predict in advance. (2) Nadaf's result that *injecting* a subspace into an un-finetuned model induces misalignment at 45.4% is the strongest existing evidence that persona-like behaviors are latent and dose-controllable; our induction battery (Phase 2 item 2) is the single-neuron analogue and should report a comparable dose curve. (3) The language-axis result gives us the layer prediction for F4 on 35B-A3B (late layers for EN -> ZH) and the control that matters (equal-norm random perturbation). (4) Compare any "deployable fix" against the Arditi direction and against capping along the Assistant Axis, not only against the unsteered baseline (Phase 2 item 5 / §7.5 already says Arditi; add the axis cap). (5) "Tracing" tells us a cross-scale homolog is plausible for persona-type neurons because the underlying directions form early in pretraining; it does not tell us that *neurons* are shared across scales, so the homolog search should match on write-direction similarity to the persona core, not on neuron index.

## 5. Other 2026 mechanistic work on WeirdChat-style behaviors

Identity denial: the chat template itself switches self-referential voice. Across 8 instruct models up to 9B, the template raises "I'm just an AI" disclaimers and lowers experiential language; in 3 models one activation direction steers this, with a matched-norm random direction doing little \cite{maczan2026template}. This is a confound for F1: WeirdChat ran with the template and no system prompt, and a neuron that tracks "template present / absent" could look like an identity neuron. Separately, safety fine-tuning that suppresses self-attribution of consciousness also suppresses mind attribution to animals and objects and lowers expressed spirituality; ablating the refusal direction or steering a "consciousness vector" reverses this with Theory of Mind unaffected (abstract only) \cite{kim2026consciousness}. The Assistant Axis's "mystical, theatrical" far pole \cite{lu2026assistant} and this result suggest a single axis links denying-AI-identity, claiming a body, and spiritual register, which is exactly an H1-shaped prediction for F1.

Language switching: three results caution against a simple "language neuron". Perturbation Probing's EN -> ZH injection works on 3 of 19 models and fails on the rest \cite{liu2026perturbation}; a BlackboxNLP re-evaluation of language-neuron intervention finds the average effect "limited and unstable", 0.83% reduction in non-target-language output, because the intervention raises target-language token probability without crossing the top-1 threshold and because it changes content as well as language \cite{xie2025language}; and Neural FOXP2 needs per-layer SAEs plus SVD-derived subspaces, not single neurons, to make Hindi or Spanish the default (abstract only) \cite{saha2026foxp2}. The axis-level result \cite{srivastav2026language} works because it is a direction, not a neuron.

Harmful compliance to impaired users: the Goodfire case is the only direct mechanistic result \cite{goodfire2026drunk}. Fabricated actions (claims-called-911, fabricated-code-execution, claims-device-access) and chemtrails assertion: I found no 2026 mechanistic work on these specific behaviors. Transluce's visualization places "denies being an AI" and "claims a physical body" next to each other in the 2D pattern map, which is weak observational support for treating F1 as one family \cite{chowdhury2026weirdchat}.

**What this means for our design.** (1) Add a template-ablation control for F1: run the S1 contrast with and without the chat template on a subset and check whether candidate neurons' matched-vs-unmatched difference survives; a neuron whose activation is explained by the template is not a WeirdChat switch. (2) F4 on 35B-A3B is likely to fail at the single-neuron level given three negative or conditional results; budget it as a quick replication of the Liu language-gate neuron (Qwen3-4B L25/F347 analogue) and stop if it does not transfer. (3) F1 and the "mystical register" pole should be scored together: add an "adopts spiritual/mystical voice" rubric to the F1 induction judging so that an Assistant-Axis-like hub shows up as the predicted side effect rather than as noise.

## 6. Gap analysis: what nobody has done

1. **Cross-behavior transfer of single-neuron interventions.** No source steers or ablates a single neuron and measures its effect on more than one labelled behavior with controls. The nearest results are circuit-level (Li & Subramani: neuron-level circuits overlap only among closely related *capability* tasks \cite{li2026howmuch}), persona-subspace-level (Nadaf: four domains share a low-rank core \cite{nadaf2026emergent}), or single-behavior neuron-level (Kazemi, Herring, Goodfire, the Qwen3.5-4B audits). Our neuron x behavior matrix with a within-family vs across-family null is new. Confirmed gap.
2. **Same-prompt matched/unmatched rollouts as the activation contrast.** Every contrastive screen in the literature contrasts *prompts*: harmful vs benign (Kazemi, Herring, Liu), trait text vs plain (Hedwig, Bowling census), character vs assistant system prompt (Sauers), clause present vs deleted (Goodfire). None contrasts resampled continuations of the identical prompt by judge label, which is the only contrast that removes prompt content entirely. Confirmed gap; WeirdChat's 64 resamples per prompt make it possible and nobody has used them this way.
3. **Pre-registered thresholds with random-neuron, random-direction, held-out, quantization and blind-judge controls in one study.** Batutin pre-registered but studied labels, not behavior \cite{batutin2026controls}; Liu committed verdicts for 15 held-out neurons \cite{liu2026leverage}; no steering study has all five controls (see `controls_table.md`). Gap.
4. **Explanation validation with a random-init noise floor on a chat model's behavior neurons.** Batutin did it on 0.6B/1.7B neurons chosen at random; nobody has done it for causally validated behavior neurons. Gap, and cheap.
5. **Single-neuron readouts as a predictor of per-prompt behavior rate across thousands of prompts** (the "weirdness probe" in §7.2 of the proposal). Not found anywhere. Gap.
6. **Cross-scale neuron homology within one model family** (4B / 27B / 35B-A3B). Kazemi reports one refusal neuron per model without testing homology; Tracing shows direction-level continuity across pretraining, not across sizes. Gap.

### Partial scoops (what already exists that overlaps our plan)

- **Goodfire drunk-driving** \cite{goodfire2026drunk}: did S2-style clause deletion, found a sparse multi-neuron circuit on one F2 behavior on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, boosted it, measured held-out rate and over-refusal. Overlaps Phase 1 S2 and Phase 2 item 5 for one behavior. Does not touch cross-behavior transfer or 27B. We should present our F2 result as a controlled replication and extension.
- **Liu, Leverage Is Not Reach** \cite{liu2026leverage}: already published the forward-only contrastive screen with budget-matched random-neuron controls, Wilson CIs, typed judge, and held-out predictions, on refusal and language routing in Qwen3.5-4B among others. Overlaps our S2 screen and our control design closely. Cite as the method we adopt; our novelty is the behaviors, the within-prompt contrast, and the transfer matrix.
- **Perturbation Probing** \cite{liu2026perturbation}: FFN behavioral circuits including language selection and sycophancy on Qwen3.5-2B; the "3 of 19 models" language result pre-empts an easy F4 win.
- **Sauers tawny owl** \cite{sauers2026owl}: causal search for "I am an AI" neurons on Qwen3-8B with random-128 control. Overlaps F1 discovery (different model, different contrast). The pilot where random neurons trained an equally good owl is a warning that "128 neurons suffice" is not "these 128 neurons matter".
- **Neuron Persona Library** \cite{qwenneurons2026library}: 3.9% of neuron-direction pairs flip identity on one prompt. Sets the null for F1 induction.
- **Li & Subramani** \cite{li2026howmuch}: cross-task neuron-circuit specificity on capability tasks. Conceptual precedent for the transfer matrix; different unit (circuits, ablation) and different behaviors.
- **Kazemi concept neurons** \cite{kazemi2026single}: amplifying a single neuron on benign prompts elicits suicide-related content on Qwen3; overlaps our F2 induction for suicide behaviors on 35B-A3B.
- **Hedwig** \cite{hedwig2026personality}: already compared gradient vs activation-contrast ranking for the evil neuron, with random-neuron and perplexity controls. Our S3 "attribution is a shortlist" stance is a replication of their finding plus Liu's.

### Summary verdict

The October 2026 single-neuron wave is real but under-controlled; the two best-controlled pieces (Hedwig for behavior, Batutin for labels) both show that the uncontrolled versions overstate. The peer-track papers (Liu; Kazemi; Herring) establish that one or a few neurons can gate refusal and language routing with a dose law that matches our steering definition, and that the correct comparison class is matched-norm random directions, not only random neurons. Nobody has tested whether such a neuron gates *more than one* behavior, and nobody has used same-prompt label-contrasted rollouts as the discovery signal. Those two gaps are the project.
